\section{The conjecture}

\begin{quote}
\textit{Definition:} the $n$-disk covering radius $r_n$ of the unit
disk is the minimal radius of $n$ equal disks covering it.
\textit{Conjecture:} $r_6$ is an explicit quadratic algebraic number
of the $(1+\sqrt{3})/2 \cdot (\sqrt{3}-1)$ type, realized by one
central disk and five symmetric annular disks; and $r_7$ is a cubic
algebraic number.
\end{quote}

\section{The refutation}

The conjecture's own displayed expression evaluates to exactly $1$:
\[
\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{2}\cdot(\sqrt{3}-1)
= \frac{(\sqrt{3}+1)(\sqrt{3}-1)}{2}
= \frac{(\sqrt{3})^2 - 1}{2} = \frac{3-1}{2} = 1 .
\]
So the claim asserts $r_6 = 1$.  But $r_6 \le 0.62 < 1$: place six
centers at the vertices of the inscribed hexagon (radius $1/2$) and
cover with radius $0.62$.  The unit disk splits into six $60^\circ$
sectors; the squared distance to the sector's center is convex on the
convex sector, so its maximum sits at a sector corner, where the
values are $1/4$ (origin) and $1 + 1/4 - \cos 30^\circ = 5/4 -
\sqrt{3}/2 \le 5/4 - 0.866 = 0.384 < 0.3844 = 0.62^2$.  Covered: the
claimed quadratic-algebraic value is refuted.  (The literature
optimum is $r_6 \approx 0.5559$, so the one-central-plus-five
configuration is not optimal either.)

\section{Verification}
