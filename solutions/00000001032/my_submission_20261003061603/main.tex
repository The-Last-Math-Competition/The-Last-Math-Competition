\section{The conjecture}

\begin{quote}
\textit{Non-cyclic} $(v,k,1)$ \textit{planar difference sets do not
exist outside} $v = q^2+q+1$ \textit{being a prime power square.}
\end{quote}

\section{The refutation}

For every $q \ge 1$,
\[
q^2 \;<\; q^2+q+1 \;<\; (q+1)^2 = q^2 + 2q + 1,
\]
since $q < 2q$. Hence $q^2+q+1$ is never a perfect square (a square
root $s$ would satisfy $q < s < q+1$ by monotonicity of squaring).
For $q = 0$, $v = 1 = 1^2$, but $1$ is not a prime power: every prime
power square is at least $4$. The conjecture's exceptional class is
therefore \emph{empty}: there is no $v = q^2+q+1$ that is a prime
power square, and the claim degenerates to ``no non-cyclic $(v,k,1)$
planar difference set exists at all.''

That is false. Non-cyclic planar difference sets exist classically:
the smallest are the $(91,10,1)$ difference sets of the
non-Desarguesian projective planes of order $9$ (Bruck; Hall). Here
$q = 9$ and $v = 91 = 9^2+9+1$, and $91 = 7 \cdot 13$ is not even a
square ($9^2 < 91 < 10^2$) --- let alone a prime power square. The
conjecture's own exceptional parameter fails to cover the known
counterexamples.

\section{Boundary}

The existence of non-cyclic $(91,10,1)$ planar difference sets is
classical finite geometry (Bruck / Hall), cited in prose; the kernel
certifies the arithmetic core --- the emptiness of the exceptional
class for all $q$ --- on which the refutation rests.
